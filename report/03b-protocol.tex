\subsection{Evaluation metrics and parameter selection}\label{sec:protocol}

% Rubric: "analyze and discuss the parameter settings".

\paragraph{Metrics.}
% [Wei Yew]: the columns below are the shared schema for every table in Section 3.3. Calinski-Harabasz
% is left out because not every member computed it.
Every result table reports the same columns. \emph{Internal} scores need no labels. The silhouette
coefficient~\cite{rousseeuw1987} of a point is $(b-a)/\max(a,b)$, where $a$ is its mean distance to its
own cluster and $b$ its mean distance to the nearest other cluster; we report the mean over points, from
$-1$ to $1$, higher is better. The Davies--Bouldin index~\cite{davies1979} averages, over clusters, the
worst ratio of within-cluster spread to between-centre distance; lower is better. Both are computed on
clustered points only, so they say nothing about the points a density method calls noise. Two
\emph{coverage} figures therefore stand next to them: the noise fraction and the share of points in the
largest cluster. \emph{External} scores need labels. The Adjusted Rand Index (ARI)~\cite{hubert1985} counts
pairs of points on which a clustering and the labels agree, corrected so that a random clustering scores
about 0 and a perfect one 1. Normalised mutual information (NMI) measures the same agreement in terms of
shared information. For both, noise counts as one more cluster, so a method cannot raise its score by
discarding hard points. We also report the fit time.

\paragraph{Which metric judges which dataset.}
% Source: experiments/08_multi_source_dataset_shape.ipynb -> results/datasets/dataset_shape.csv
% (src/sc4020/datasets/describe_dataset.py). Labels describe the classes only; no method is tuned on them.
No single score suits every dataset, so before clustering we measured each dataset's shape
(Table~\ref{tab:metric_choice}). Density spread is the 90th over the 10th percentile of each point's distance
to its 10th neighbour (1 means even density). Far outliers are points whose distance lies beyond
$Q_3+3\,\mathrm{IQR}$; this share is the noise a dataset can be expected to have, so a noise fraction well
above it means a method discarded real points rather than removed outliers. With labels we also score the
true classes as if they were a clustering, and compare two classifiers: 1-nearest-neighbour follows any
shape, nearest-centroid only convex, roughly spherical classes, so a 1-NN accuracy well above the
nearest-centroid accuracy means the classes are not convex. Three rules follow. (i) On labelled data, ARI
ranks the methods and NMI confirms it. (ii) The silhouette and Davies--Bouldin assume compact, convex
clusters. They are trusted only when the true classes have that shape (D6), and are never trusted when the
true classes themselves score worse than a wrong clustering (D1: 0.387 for the moons against 0.495 for
K-means). (iii) On unlabelled data they are the only scores we have, so they are read only together with the
fake-data silhouette, the noise fraction against the far-outlier share, and the largest cluster.

\paragraph{Fake-data check.}
An internal score has no fixed scale: a silhouette of 0.3 can be strong on one dataset and meaningless on
another. Each chosen setting is therefore run again on fake data that keeps each feature's distribution but
has no joint structure: every column shuffled independently (seed 0), on every dataset. A result counts
as structure only if the real data score clearly above the fake data.

\paragraph{Selection rule.}
% Sources: results/datasets/make_moons_tuning.csv (DBSCAN eps 0.089, minPts 16);
% results/datasets/fashion_mnist_tuning.csv (DBSCAN eps 2.94, minPts 40).
One rule picks the setting of DBSCAN and HDBSCAN on every dataset: among the settings that give at least
2 clusters and label at most 50\% of points as noise, take the one with the highest silhouette. The noise
cap is needed because the silhouette ignores noise. Without it, the silhouette prefers settings that throw
most of the data away. On D1 it would pick DBSCAN with $\varepsilon=0.089$, which scores 0.948 by calling
96.5\% of points noise; on D5, DBSCAN with $\varepsilon=2.94$, which scores 0.525 with 90.4\% noise.
For K-means, $K$ is the number of classes on labelled data; otherwise, among the $K$ whose 10 seeds agree
(pairwise ARI $\geq0.95$), the one with the largest gap between the real silhouette and the mean
silhouette on 3 fake copies. GMM takes the components and covariance type with the lowest BIC, with the
number of components fixed to the number of classes on labelled data. Spectral takes the $K$ of K-means.
On labelled data we also record the setting with the best ARI, so the cost of choosing without labels is
visible.

\paragraph{Parameter grids.}
% Source: src/sc4020/tuning.py, src/sc4020/run.py; every setting tried is in results/datasets/<dataset>_tuning.csv.
The same grids are used on every dataset. K-means and K-means++: $K=2$ to 10 with 10 starts (on D2 a
coarse grid from 2 to 50, since a global catalogue has dozens of belts). GMM: the same $K$ grid and four
covariance types (full, tied, diagonal, spherical). DBSCAN: \texttt{minPts} at half, once, twice and four
times $m=\mathrm{clip}(2d,4,20)$, and $\varepsilon$ at 0.5 to 4 times the knee of the k-distance curve.
HDBSCAN: \texttt{min\_cluster\_size} in $\{5,10,15,20,30,50,100,200,400\}$ up to $n/10$, and
\texttt{min\_samples} in \{default, 5, 10\}. Spectral: an RBF affinity on D6 and a 10-nearest-neighbour
graph elsewhere. Silhouettes are estimated on a sample of 5{,}000 points when $n$ is larger.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.}

Every method uses seed 42 and the fake data seed 0. All seven notebooks ran on Python 3.13.15,
scikit-learn~\cite{pedregosa2011} 1.9.1, NumPy 2.5.3 and pandas 3.0.5, on an AMD Ryzen 7 5800H with
16\,GB of memory.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
% Every number is quoted from Section 3.
We compared six clustering methods, K-means with random and K-means++ starts, GMM, spectral clustering,
DBSCAN and HDBSCAN, on seven datasets, each chosen to test one data property, with every setting chosen by
one label-free rule. Which family wins depends on the data. Density and graph methods win when groups are
separated by gaps and have any shape, in few dimensions: on D1 spectral clustering recovers the moons
exactly where K-means reaches ARI 0.48, and on D2 HDBSCAN follows belts that K-means merges. Centroid and
mixture methods win when classes are compact and touch, or the data have many dimensions: on D4 GMM reaches
ARI 0.618 and K-Means++ 0.508 while both density methods fall to 0.04, and on D5 no density setting
reaches 0.07. Where the data have no gaps at all, as in the core
of D3 and in D7, no family finds natural groups. Density methods return one dominant cluster, spectral
clustering scores below its own fake data, and K-means draws borders where its objective puts them.

The silhouette did not tell us which case we were in. On four of the five labelled datasets the method
with the top silhouette was not the one that matched the labels best; on D4 it ranks the six methods
almost in reverse (Spearman $\rho=-0.82$); and on D1 it chose a DBSCAN setting that shattered the moons
when another setting recovered them exactly. Rerunning each result on fake data helps where a method finds
nothing real (D4, D7), but not where it finds the wrong structure (D2). Adding one component to a method, namely
K-means++ seeding, HDBSCAN's density hierarchy, GMM's covariances and spectral clustering's neighbour
graph, repaired the failures it targets, each at a cost, and none produced clusters in data with no gaps. The
choice of method should follow from what the data look like and what the analysis needs, and every score
should be checked against fake data and against the points it ignores.

\subsection*{Limitations}
D2 and D3 have no labels, so their clusters are judged against plate-boundary geography and the map of
the city only. Density parameters such as \texttt{minPts} and \texttt{min\_cluster\_size} are counts of
points, so the clusters change with the size of the data: a random half of the 2023 earthquakes agrees
with the full year at ARI 0.51 to 0.55 for the density methods, against 0.97 to 0.99 for K-Means++.
% Source: results/earthquakes_2023/ablation_time_window.csv (random-half control rows).
D7 has no location beyond the town, so its clusters describe flats rather
than places, and its one label, flat type, is nearly a function of floor area, so a low ARI on D7 says
little about the quality of a clustering.

On the labelled datasets, K-means, GMM and spectral clustering were given the number of classes, which an
analyst would not know, and the best-ARI rows use the labels outright; both overstate what can be reached
without labels. D4 and D6 are small (336 and 178 points), so a single protein moves a rare class's share
by up to 50\%. The datasets keep their owners' parameter grids, so the rule is shared but the search space
is not. On D1 and D6 the fake-data setting is chosen again on the fake data, and on D5 the fake-data check
was not run, because its data could not be rebuilt identically outside the owner's environment.

Our evidence on dimensionality comes from two datasets, D4 with 7 features and D5 reduced to 50
principal components from 784 pixels, which keep 86.5\% of the variance. We did not test the methods on
the full 784 dimensions or beyond, so the high-dimensional results describe data that have already been
reduced.

\subsection{Ablation of method components}\label{sec:ablation}

% Rubric: "ablation study of the key components". One change at a time, everything else fixed.

An ablation removes or swaps one component of a method and keeps everything else fixed. Four of our
methods extend a simpler one by a single component, so each is compared with its simpler form at the
settings of Section~ef{sec:comparison}: K-Means++ adds a seeding rule to K-means (A), HDBSCAN adds a
density hierarchy to DBSCAN (B), GMM adds a covariance per cluster to K-means (C), and spectral
clustering depends on the graph it is given (D).

\subsubsection{A: Initialisation (random vs K-Means++ seeding)}
% Sources: results/hotspot/ablation.csv (rows ablation=init), init_seeds.csv, case_stats.csv (gaps, seeds
% with ARI >= 0.7); figure results/hotspot/figures/f6_init_d3d4.png.
On D3 ($K=5$) and D4 ($K=8$) each initialisation ran from one start with 30 seeds, and each run is measured
by how far its inertia lies above the best of all 60 runs. On D4, K-Means++ moves
almost every start close to the best: the mean gap falls from 52.9\% to 3.4\% and the worst from 69.4\%
to 12.4\%, and the mean iterations fall from 15.6 to 11.1. ARI rises from $0.430\pm0.071$ to
$0.508\pm0.076$ (medians 0.42 and 0.50), but only a few starts of either kind reach ARI above 0.7
(Figure~\ref{fig:init}), and K-Means++ reaches the high group in only 2 of 30 seeds against 1 of 30. It
raises the chance of a good start; one start is still not enough. On D3, 29 of 30 starts with either
method reach the best inertia and one lands 28\% above it; only the iterations fall, from 22.7 to 15.5,
and two runs agree at ARI 0.96 with random starts and 0.98 with K-Means++.

% Sources: results/earthquakes_2023/kmeans_init_ablation_summary.csv;
% results/hdb_resale/kmeans_init_ablation.csv (gap_to_best_% per init, mean n_iter).
On D2 and D7 each initialisation ran from one start with 20 seeds at the chosen $K$. On D2 ($K=6$)
K-Means++ helps clearly: the mean gap falls from 10.9\% to 4.8\%, the worst from 18.8\% to 11.5\%, and
iterations from 15.3 to 10.3. It does not remove the need for restarts. Two K-Means++ runs still agree
only at ARI 0.80 on average, against 0.78 for random starts, because the belts give many local optima of
similar inertia. On D7 ($K=7$) the choice matters little: the mean gap is 2.3\% with random starts and
1.3\% with K-Means++, and the worst about 4\% for both, though K-Means++ needs fewer iterations (23.5
against 34.9). Initialisation matters when the objective has many local optima of different quality, as
on D2 and D4; where every start reaches nearly the same one, it does not.

\subsubsection{B: The density hierarchy (DBSCAN vs HDBSCAN)}\label{sec:abl_hierarchy}
% Sources: Tables tab:cmp_d2d7, tab:cmp_d3d4, tab:cmp_labelled; results/earthquakes_2023/case_density_regions.csv.
HDBSCAN is DBSCAN with one component added: instead of one radius for the whole dataset, it builds the
hierarchy of all radii and keeps, in each region, the cluster that persists longest
(Section~\ref{sec:methods}). Table~\ref{tab:abl_hierarchy} sets the two side by side at the settings the
rule chose on each dataset. The hierarchy helps where density differs between clusters. On D2 it clusters
62\% of the Americas instead of 20\% and calls less of the data noise overall (30.7\% against 40.4\%); on
D1 it avoids the 72 fragments DBSCAN settles on (ARI 0.009 to 0.709); on D3 it separates LaGuardia from
the city. It does not help where there are no gaps at any density: on D4 both pick the same partition, and
on D5 and D7 ARI stays below 0.08. On D6 it is worse (0.431 to 0.260), because it calls 42\% of the wines
noise. Its cost is time: it is slower on every dataset, by a factor of about 24 on D2.

\subsubsection{C: A covariance per cluster (K-Means++ vs GMM)}\label{sec:abl_covariance}
% Sources: Tables tab:cmp_d2d7, tab:cmp_d3d4, tab:cmp_labelled (K-Means++ and GMM rows, rule's K).
A Gaussian mixture with equal spherical covariances and hard assignments reduces to K-means; the component
GMM adds is a covariance matrix per cluster, which lets clusters differ in size, shape and orientation
(Section~\ref{sec:methods}). At the same $K$ on every dataset, the covariance raises ARI on four of the
five labelled datasets, most on D4 (0.508 to 0.618) and D5 (0.343 to 0.389), and slightly on D1 and D6
(0.481 to 0.501, 0.897 to 0.915); on D7 it lowers it (0.173 to 0.158). It does not repair a wrong shape:
on D1 both still cut the moons. And the silhouette falls on every dataset except D1, where it is unchanged
(on D4 from 0.260 to 0.157), so an analyst choosing by the silhouette would remove the component exactly
where it helps most.

\subsubsection{D: The affinity graph (spectral clustering on D6)}\label{sec:abl_affinity}
% Sources: results/datasets/wine_results.csv (Spectral, affinity rbf); results/comparison/report_d6_d7.csv
% (Spectral, nearest-neighbour graph), both at K=3 on the same standardised data.
Spectral clustering partitions whatever graph it is given, so the affinity is its key component. On D6 it
ran twice at $K=3$ on the same standardised data: once with a dense RBF kernel at scikit-learn's default
bandwidth ($\gamma=1$), and once with the 10-nearest-neighbour graph used on every other dataset. ARI is
0.445 with the kernel and 0.880 with the graph (silhouette 0.249 and 0.283). The likely reason is scale:
with 13 standardised features most pairs of wines are several units apart, so a kernel with $\gamma=1$
gives them almost no affinity and the graph carries little of the class structure, while the
neighbour graph adapts to the local distances. The same graph is what lets spectral
clustering recover the moons of D1 exactly (Section~\ref{sec:shape}).

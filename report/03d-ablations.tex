\subsubsection{A: Initialisation (K-Means vs K-Means++)}
% [Ky] Sources: SC4020-Ky/results/ablation.csv (rows ablation=init), init_seeds.csv (one row per seed).
On D3 ($K=5$) and D4 ($K=7$) each initialisation ran from one start with 30 seeds. On D4, K-Means++
lowers the mean inertia by 25\% (845 to 632) and the mean iterations from 17.7 to 11.0, and raises ARI
from $0.485\pm0.095$ to $0.561\pm0.104$. That gain is a third of the gap between the two main runs
(10 starts, seed 42: ARI 0.493 against 0.731), so the larger figure rests on one seed. On D3 the mean
inertia is the same for both (within 0.01\%); only the iterations fall, from 22.7 to 15.5, and two runs
agree at ARI 0.96 with random starts and 0.98 with K-Means++.

% [Ky] Gaps and the count of seeds with ARI >= 0.7: SC4020-Ky/results/case_stats.csv (Ablation A rows);
% figure from SC4020-Ky/src/figures.py (F6b).
Table~\ref{tab:init} measures each run the same way as the D2 and D8 runs below: how far its inertia lies
above the best of all 60 runs. K-Means++ moves most D4 starts close to the best (mean gap 8.9\% against 45.7\%),
but its worst start is as bad as the worst random one (55\%). Figure~\ref{fig:init} shows why the ARI
gain is modest. The starts fall into two groups, one near ARI 0.73 and one near 0.5, and K-Means++
reaches the high group in 8 of 30 seeds against 3 of 30. It raises the chance of a good start, but one
start is still not enough. On D3, 29 of 30 starts with either method reach the best inertia, and one
lands 28\% above it.

On D2 and D8 each initialisation ran from one start with 20 seeds at the chosen $K$; we measure how far
each run's inertia lies above the best run. On D2 ($K=6$) K-Means++ helps clearly: the mean gap falls from
10.9\% to 4.8\%, the worst from 18.8\% to 11.5\%, and iterations from 15.3 to 10.3. It does not remove the
need for restarts. Two K-Means++ runs still agree only at ARI 0.80 on average, against 0.78 for random
starts, because the belts give many local optima of similar inertia. On D8 ($K=4$) the choice does not
matter: all 40 runs end within 0.009\% of the best inertia. Initialisation matters when the objective has
many local optima of different quality; on D8 every start reaches practically the same one.

\subsubsection{B: Distance on geographic coordinates}
% Source: weiyew/sc4020-earthquake/results/ablation_distance.csv; figure from
% weiyew/sc4020-earthquake/notebooks/ablation.ipynb, section 2.
Every method ran at its chosen setting twice on D2: once on the sphere (Section~\ref{sec:prep}) and once
on latitude and longitude as flat numbers (DBSCAN's $\varepsilon$ converted at 111.2\,km per degree). To
isolate the effect of the $180^\circ$ meridian we count \emph{cross-line pairs}: the 92 pairs of
earthquakes within 50\,km of each other on opposite sides of the line. Table~\ref{tab:abl_sphere} shows that flat
coordinates separate every one of these pairs, for every method (Figure~\ref{fig:abl_sphere}). The
Fiji--Tonga--Kermadec system is cut in two. K-Means++ changes everywhere: its silhouette falls from 0.528 to
0.404 and its partition agrees with the sphere partition at ARI 0.557 only. DBSCAN hides the failure in
its global numbers. Its silhouette moves from 0.703 to 0.701 and its partition still agrees at ARI 0.948,
because only the clusters that cross the line are split. A check aimed at the known problem, here the
cross-line count, finds what the internal scores miss.

\subsubsection{C: Feature scaling}
% [Ky] Source: SC4020-Ky/results/ablation.csv (rows ablation=scaling).
On D4, standardising the seven features raises K-Means++ ARI ($K=7$) from 0.497 to 0.731
(Figure~\ref{fig:abl}, right). DBSCAN, given its best $\varepsilon$ in each case, moves slightly the other
way, from 0.481 to 0.455. K-means sums squared distances, so until the features are scaled the ones with
the widest range decide the partition. DBSCAN only counts neighbours within $\varepsilon$, and re-tuning
$\varepsilon$ absorbs most of a change of scale.

% Source: weiyew/sc4020-hdb/results/kmeans_sensitivity.csv; price share from
% weiyew/sc4020-hdb/notebooks/eda.ipynb, section 9.
On D8, K-means ($K=4$) on raw units scores a silhouette of 0.540, well above the 0.317 of the standardised
run. The higher score is not a better clustering. In dollars, price makes up 100.0\% of the squared
distance between sales, so the four clusters are price bands and floor area, lease and storey play no part.
The raw partition agrees with the standardised one at ARI 0.261 only, and neither matches flat type
(ARI 0.229 raw, 0.214 standardised). Scaling decides which question the clustering answers. The
silhouette, measured in each run's own units, cannot say which is right.

\subsubsection{D: Dimensionality}
% [Ky] Source: SC4020-Ky/results/ablation.csv (rows ablation=dimensionality).
We project D4 onto its first 2, 3, 4 and 5 principal components, keep all 7 features as the last case,
and tune both methods again in each: DBSCAN with \texttt{minPts} 5 and the $\varepsilon$ with the best ARI,
K-Means++ with $K=7$ (Table~\ref{tab:dim}). DBSCAN leads at 2 and 3 components, and K-Means++ from 4 on.
DBSCAN peaks at 3 components and then falls, while K-Means++ rises from 4 components to 7, where it leads
by 0.276. As components are added, distances between points become more alike~\cite{beyer1999}, so a
radius separates dense from sparse less well, while the added components still carry class information
that K-means can use. The viable-$\varepsilon$ column shows a second effect. On 2 components only 12.5\%
of the $\varepsilon$ grid gives at least 2 clusters with under 50\% noise, so DBSCAN's best result there
would be hard to find without labels. We vary the number of PCA components, not the intrinsic dimension
of the data, which we do not know.

\subsubsection{E: Values recorded in steps}
% Sources: weiyew/sc4020-hdb/results/ablation_largest_cluster_cap.csv, ablation_capped_contents.csv,
% agreement.csv; figure from weiyew/sc4020-hdb/notebooks/ablation.ipynb.
On D8 the selection rule picks density settings dominated by one cluster: it holds 97.1\% of sales for
DBSCAN, and 68.6\% for HDBSCAN, which calls another 28.5\% noise (Section~\ref{sec:nogaps}). A natural repair is to add a cap on the largest
cluster. We tested caps from 90\% down to 50\% on the data as recorded and on jittered data
(Section~\ref{sec:prep}). On recorded data a cap of 50\% does give balanced partitions, but of the wrong
kind: all 27 DBSCAN clusters, and 6 of the 7 HDBSCAN clusters, each contain a single storey band
(Figure~\ref{fig:abl_ties}). They are stacks of identical recorded values, not groups of similar flats. On
jittered data, where those ties are spread out, \emph{no} setting meets a cap of 50\% or 60\%. The balanced
partitions existed only because of the recording steps. At the uncapped HDBSCAN setting, by contrast,
recorded and jittered data agree (ARI 0.886), so the chosen setting is not a tie artefact. Two lessons
follow. Stepped values must be checked before a density method runs. And a constraint added to a
selection rule can make it pick artefacts, which is why we kept the rule unchanged.

\begin{figure}[h]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{hdb_failure_ties}
\caption{D8, HDBSCAN chosen under a 50\% largest-cluster cap. Left, recorded values: 6 of 7 clusters are
single storey bands. Right, the same setting on jittered values: 2 clusters. Cause: storeys recorded in
3-storey bands create exact ties that density methods read as dense regions.}

\end{figure}

\subsubsection{F: Data subsets}
% Sources: weiyew/sc4020-earthquake/results/ablation_time_window.csv, ablation_min_magnitude.csv.
Each D2 subset is compared with a random subset of the same size, so that an effect of the subset is not
confused with an effect of size. Clustering January to June or July to December separately, K-Means++
reproduces its full-year labels at ARI 0.963 and 0.829 (random halves: 0.968 and 0.994); the drop for the
second half comes from aftershock sequences. Density methods change as much on random halves as on the
real ones (HDBSCAN 0.414 and 0.472, against 0.505 and 0.544). Their parameters are counts of points, so
their output depends on how many points there are. Keeping only magnitude 5.0 and above (1{,}780
earthquakes) behaves like a random subset of the same size (DBSCAN ARI 0.343 against 0.315, HDBSCAN 0.322
against 0.303). Larger earthquakes do not outline the belts more clearly, so we keep the 4.5 threshold.

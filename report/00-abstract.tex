\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Clustering is used when there are no labels to check the result against, so a clustering is usually
judged by an internal score such as the silhouette. We test whether that is safe. The methods assume
different things: K-means and Gaussian mixtures expect compact groups, spectral clustering expects groups
joined by chains of close neighbours, and DBSCAN and HDBSCAN expect dense groups separated by empty
space. We ran all six (K-means with random and K-means++ starts, GMM, spectral clustering, DBSCAN and
HDBSCAN) on seven datasets chosen to break these assumptions, five of them with known labels, and chose
every setting with one label-free rule. We asked three questions: does a method fail when its assumption
breaks, can the failure be seen without labels, and does extending a method by one component fix it? Every method failed where
its assumption broke. K-means cut two curved clusters in the wrong place (agreement with the true labels
0.48 out of 1, against 1.00 for spectral clustering), and on housing data with no empty space between
groups DBSCAN put 96\% of sales in one cluster. The silhouette did not reveal these failures: on four of
the five labelled datasets it favoured a different method from the one that best matched the labels, and
on the curved clusters it chose a DBSCAN setting that shattered them into 72 pieces (agreement 0.01).
Comparing each result with the same setting on fake data caught results with no real structure, but not
K-means joining earthquake belts thousands of kilometres apart into one cluster. Adding one component
to a method, such as HDBSCAN's density hierarchy or GMM's covariances, fixed the failures it targets,
but none helped data with no gaps. No single score can choose a clustering method: an analyst should look at the data, report
how many points each method discards, and test directly for the failures the data make likely.
\end{abstract}

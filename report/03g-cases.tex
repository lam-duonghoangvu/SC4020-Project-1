\paragraph{Spectral and DBSCAN on D1 (Lam).} % lam/results/make_moons_results.csv
Both follow the curve of each moon (Figure~\ref{fig:lam-d1}): Spectral reaches ARI 1.000 and DBSCAN
0.982, leaving 9 outlying points as noise. They succeed for the same reason: each moon is a chain of
close neighbours, and the gap between moons is wider than any link in the chain. K-Means++ and GMM
fail on the same data because they assume convex clusters.

\begin{figure}[h]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{lam_d1_moons_grid}
\caption{D1 make\_moons. K-Means++ and GMM cut both moons with a straight boundary (ARI 0.48, 0.49);
DBSCAN, HDBSCAN and Spectral follow the curves (ARI 0.98, 0.95, 1.00). Grey crosses: noise.}
\label{fig:lam-d1}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{DBSCAN on D5 (Lam).} % lam/results/fashion_mnist_results.csv
DBSCAN puts most of the ten classes into one cluster, a second small cluster at the edge, and 33\%
of images into noise (ARI 0.033; Figure~\ref{fig:lam-d5}). The cause is one global $\varepsilon$ in 50
dimensions: an $\varepsilon$ large enough to connect points within a class also connects the
overlapping classes to each other, and a smaller one turns most points into noise. The $k$-based
methods, which are not tied to a density threshold, reach ARI 0.34--0.40 on the same data.

\begin{figure}[h]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{lam_d5_fashion_grid}
\caption{D5 Fashion-MNIST (3{,}000 images; clustered in 50 PCA dimensions, plotted in the first two).
DBSCAN merges most classes into one cluster (ARI 0.03); HDBSCAN calls 71\% of points noise (ARI 0.05).
Spectral, GMM and K-Means++ reach ARI 0.40, 0.39, 0.34.}
\label{fig:lam-d5}
\end{figure}

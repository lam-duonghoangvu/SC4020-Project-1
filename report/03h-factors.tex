first three factors decide \emph{which family} of method can work at all, and no parameter setting
overrides them. The rest change how well a suitable method does.

\paragraph{Practical guidance.}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
\item \textbf{Choose by the structure of the data, not by a score.} Plot the data or a projection first.
If the groups are curved or long, no choice of $k$ will help K-means or GMM; if they are compact and
touching, or the data have more than a handful of dimensions, no choice of $\varepsilon$ will help DBSCAN.
\item \textbf{Do not pick settings by the silhouette alone.} It favours few, round clusters, small
fragments and settings that call hard points noise. First restrict the search to what the analysis needs
(a noise cap, a minimum number of clusters), then compare within that range.
\item \textbf{Report the noise fraction and the largest cluster next to every silhouette.} A silhouette
computed after discarding half the points, or with almost all of them in one cluster, says little.
\item \textbf{Compare every result against fake data.} Rerun the chosen setting on shuffled columns or
uniform points. It catches results with no real structure, though not results with the wrong structure.
\item \textbf{Check for values recorded in steps.} Tied values form dense spots that density methods
report as clusters; add jitter within each step and see whether the clusters remain.
\item \textbf{Standardise before K-means, and use K-means++ with several starts.} One start is not enough
when the objective has many local optima.
\item \textbf{Prefer a parameter whose unit means something, and measure distance correctly.} A radius
in kilometres can be checked by someone who knows the area; a $k$ cannot. Use distance on the sphere for
global coordinates; a local projection is enough within a city.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Curved and elongated clusters}\label{sec:shape}
% Source: results/comparison/report_d1.csv; best-ARI density settings from results/datasets/make_moons_tuning.csv.
On D1 the centroid methods fail and the graph method succeeds. K-Means++ (ARI 0.481) and GMM (0.501) both
draw a straight boundary that cuts each moon in two, giving the tip of one moon to the other cluster
(Figure~\ref{fig:lam-d1}). Spectral clustering with a 10-nearest-neighbour graph recovers both moons
exactly (ARI 1.000). The density methods can do so too: their best settings by ARI reach 1.000. But the
rule does not pick them. It picks a DBSCAN radius so small that the moons break into 72 fragments with
47\% of points as noise (ARI 0.009), and an HDBSCAN setting that drops the sparse moon tips as noise
(15.6\%, ARI 0.709). The silhouette ranks the methods almost the other way round: HDBSCAN 0.529, DBSCAN
0.523, K-Means++ and GMM 0.495, and spectral clustering last at 0.387, because it rewards compact, convex
groups, which is exactly the wrong assumption here.

% Source: experiments/09_usgs_earthquakes_2023_ablation.ipynb (K-means belt case, centre distances).
K-means assumes compact clusters, and D2 breaks that assumption at planetary scale. Earthquakes follow
plate boundaries, so a cluster is a long, narrow belt rather than a ball around a centre. K-Means++ with
$K=6$ must cover the whole globe with six roughly round regions, so each region takes in several
unrelated belts (Figure~\ref{fig:kmeans_belts}). One cluster of 905 earthquakes joins Turkey, the Reykjanes
Ridge and Iceland, Afghanistan and Tajikistan, Somalia and Eritrea, and the Mid-Indian Ridge. HDBSCAN finds
each of these as its own cluster, and their centres are 2{,}932 to 11{,}449\,km apart. K-means also has no
noise label, so every isolated earthquake in the oceans is assigned to some region. Its silhouette is
still 0.529, well above the 0.390 of shuffled coordinates: the score confirms that the data are not
random, not that the clusters are meaningful. GMM and spectral clustering, held to the same six clusters,
score lower (0.407 and 0.333); with six clusters for the 28 belts HDBSCAN finds, every one of them must
also join several belts.

\subsubsection{Uneven density}\label{sec:density}
% Sources: results/earthquakes_2023/case_density_regions.csv, method_comparison.csv.
On D2 the distance to the nearest earthquake runs from 1.2\,km to 100.5\,km (5th to 95th percentile), and
the k-distance curve rises smoothly with no knee. Any single $\varepsilon$ is therefore too small for some
belts and too large for others. At the chosen $\varepsilon=101.8$\,km, DBSCAN clusters 90\% of the
earthquakes in the dense south-west Pacific but only 20\% in the sparser Americas
(Figure~\ref{fig:density}). HDBSCAN, which picks a density level per cluster, clusters 62\% of the
Americas and keeps the Mexico to Panama belt and the Chile and Argentina belt as long clusters. The price
is paid in the dense region, where it clusters 78\% instead of 90\%. Overall HDBSCAN keeps more
earthquakes (69.3\% against 59.6\%) with a higher silhouette (0.758 against 0.703,
Table~\ref{tab:cmp_d2d7}).

\begin{figure}[h]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{eq_case_density_dbscan_vs_hdbscan}
\caption{D2, DBSCAN ($\varepsilon=101.8$\,km) against HDBSCAN in a sparse region (top, the Americas)
and a dense one (bottom, the south-west Pacific); grey points are noise. Success of HDBSCAN on the sparse
belts, at the cost of trimming the dense ones. Cause: density varies along and between belts.}

\end{figure}

% Sources: results/hotspot/case_stats.csv (cluster shares and centres), sweep.csv (knee setting),
% shared_rule.csv; results/comparison/gmm_spectral.csv.
On D3 density varies as much, but inside one connected mass. Lower and midtown Manhattan, the rest of
Manhattan, Brooklyn and western Queens meet without a gap, so the density methods keep them together.
DBSCAN at the knee of the k-distance curve ($\varepsilon=0.363$\,km, \texttt{minPts}~4) returns 48
clusters, yet 81.4\% of pickups sit in the first and 9.6\% in a second one over Brooklyn; the rest are the
airports and dozens of small spots, and the silhouette is $-0.07$. The rule prefers a radius four times
larger, $\varepsilon=1.53$\,km: two clusters, the city (95.9\%) and JFK, with a silhouette of 0.779
(Table~\ref{tab:cmp_d3d4}). HDBSCAN finds the city, JFK and LaGuardia (91.2\%, 2.0\% and 1.9\%; 4.8\%
noise). The airports are the only parts of D3 with a gap around them, and both density methods find them.
K-means ($K=5$) does divide the core, into southern Manhattan (44.5\%), northern Manhattan with the Bronx
(39.9\%) and Brooklyn (9.3\%), plus the two airport areas, but those borders follow the K-means objective,
not gaps in the data. Its silhouette is 0.481 against 0.362 on shuffled coordinates, so the split is not
random, but nothing in the data marks where it should fall. Spectral clustering, which cuts the
neighbour graph where it is thinnest, scores below its own fake data (0.155 against 0.291). The likely
reason is that a mass with no thin places gives it nowhere natural to cut.

\subsubsection{High dimensions}\label{sec:dims}
% Sources: results/hotspot/shared_rule.csv, ablation.csv (dimensionality); results/comparison/gmm_spectral.csv.
On D4 (seven dimensions) the methods that need $K$ do best: GMM (ARI 0.618), K-Means++ (0.508) and
spectral clustering (0.392) at the number of classes. The density methods do far worse. The rule picks
settings for DBSCAN and HDBSCAN that give the same partition, one cluster holding 97\% of proteins and
ARI 0.038 (Table~\ref{tab:cmp_d3d4}), and their silhouette (0.559) is barely above the same settings on
shuffled columns (0.545 and 0.551). The fake-data check flags both. Even with the labels, the best DBSCAN
setting reaches only 0.479, by calling a quarter of the proteins noise.

% Source: results/datasets/fashion_mnist_results.csv, fashion_mnist_tuning.csv.
On D5 ($d=50$) the gap is wider. Spectral (ARI 0.398), GMM (0.389) and K-Means++ (0.343) all fall well
short of the labels because shirts, T-shirts, pullovers and coats overlap. DBSCAN finds two clusters and
23\% noise (ARI 0.028): one cluster absorbs three quarters of the images. HDBSCAN also finds two clusters, with 33\% noise (ARI 0.023). Even the best
density settings by ARI reach only 0.034 and 0.066, because in 50 dimensions the k-distances of all points
are similar and there is no density gap between the overlapping classes for one threshold to find.

\subsubsection{No gaps between groups}\label{sec:nogaps}
% Sources: results/hdb_resale/comparison.csv, kmeans_k_sweep.csv; results/comparison/gmm_spectral.csv.
D7 is one continuous mass: flats come in every combination of size, lease, storey and price, with no empty
space between groups. Table~\ref{tab:cmp_d2d7} shows what each method does with it. K-means still finds a
weak partition into seven segments, with a silhouette of 0.301 against 0.191 on shuffled columns. That
ratio of 1.58 is modest, but it is the clearest result on D7 that beats its fake data; GMM's 0.126 against
0.084 is weaker, and spectral clustering scores below zero on both. Density methods have no sparse region
to cut along. The DBSCAN setting chosen by the rule puts 96.0\% of sales in one cluster, and on shuffled
columns the same setting forms a single cluster too. HDBSCAN finds one core holding 65.6\% of sales and
calls 31.6\% noise, and it scores far below the same setting on shuffled columns (0.158 against 0.564). On
the recorded values, without the jitter of Section~\ref{sec:prep}, HDBSCAN instead returns 37 clusters, 34
of them single storey bands. On data with no gaps, a density method has nothing to find, and
the selection rule cannot tell.

\subsubsection{Silhouette versus ground truth}\label{sec:silhouette}
% Sources: Tables tab:cmp_d2d7, tab:cmp_d3d4 and tab:cmp_labelled; Spearman from results/hotspot/shared_rule.csv
% and results/comparison/gmm_spectral.csv (six methods, settings chosen by the rule).
On four of the five labelled datasets the method with the highest silhouette is not the one that matches
the labels best. On D1 the top silhouette goes to HDBSCAN (0.529) and the best ARI to spectral clustering
(1.000, silhouette 0.387). On D4 the silhouette ranks the six methods almost exactly opposite to ARI
(Spearman $\rho=-0.82$; Figure~\ref{fig:silhouette} shows the four main ones): the two density methods top
it with the worst ARI. On D5 K-Means++ has the top silhouette (0.202) and spectral clustering the best ARI
(0.398). D7 is the only exception, and a weak one: K-means with random starts has both the top silhouette
(0.301) and the top ARI against flat type (0.223). In each case the silhouette favours a result that
discards points or draws compact groups, not the one that follows the classes.

\paragraph{D6 Wine and the fake-data check.}
% Source: results/comparison/report_d6_d7.csv (wine rows).
D6 repeats the pattern inside the noise cap. DBSCAN and HDBSCAN pass the cap with a quarter and two fifths
of the wines as noise, take the two highest silhouettes (0.365 and 0.363) and the two lowest ARI (0.431 and
0.260), while GMM, K-Means++ and spectral clustering reach 0.915, 0.897 and 0.880 with silhouettes near
0.28. The fake-data check does not separate good from bad here either. Every method that assigns all
points scores far above its shuffled-column silhouette (0.285 against 0.066 for K-Means++), and the density
methods form fewer than two clusters on shuffled data. So each result passes the check, including the
density results that match the labels worst: what they find, a dense core of one class, is real
structure, but it is not the class structure. The check shows that a result is not an artefact of the
marginals. It does not show which of several real structures a method has found.

\subsubsection{Curved clusters}\label{sec:shape}
% Sources: results/datasets/make_moons_results.csv; make_moons_tuning.csv (best-ARI DBSCAN setting).
On D1 the methods that assume convex clusters fail and the graph method succeeds
(Figure~\ref{fig:d1}). K-Means++ (ARI 0.481) and GMM (0.501) draw a nearly straight boundary that cuts
each moon in two. Spectral clustering on a 10-nearest-neighbour graph recovers both moons exactly (ARI
1.000), because each moon is a chain of close neighbours with a gap wider than any link between the
moons. HDBSCAN finds both moons too but calls 15.6\% of points noise, mostly the sparse moon tips, so its
ARI is 0.709. DBSCAN fails, and not because of its assumptions: its grid contains a setting that recovers
the moons exactly ($\varepsilon=0.229$, \texttt{minPts}~3, ARI 1.000), but the selection rule prefers
$\varepsilon=0.049$, which breaks the moons into 72 fragments with 47.1\% noise (ARI 0.009), because the
fragments score a higher silhouette (0.523 against 0.387).

\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{make_moons_clusters}
\caption{D1 make\_moons at the chosen settings. K-means and GMM cut both moons with a straight boundary;
Spectral follows the curves (ARI 1.000); HDBSCAN loses the tips to noise; DBSCAN at the radius the
silhouette prefers breaks the moons into 72 fragments. Grey crosses: noise.}
\label{fig:d1}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Long belts of uneven density}\label{sec:density}
% Sources: results/datasets/earthquakes_2023_results.csv; figure earthquakes_2023_clusters.png.
On D2 clusters should follow plate boundaries: long, narrow belts whose density varies along and
between belts. HDBSCAN, which sets a density level per cluster, returns 28 clusters that follow the belts
(largest 7.5\% of earthquakes) and calls 30.7\% noise; its silhouette is the highest on D2 (0.756, against
0.317 on fake data). DBSCAN with one radius ($\approx$250\,km) must trade the sparse belts against the
dense ones: it keeps more earthquakes (11.3\% noise) but chains the dense western Pacific into one
cluster holding 35.7\% of all earthquakes. K-Means++ and Spectral ($K=6$) cover the globe with six
regions, each joining several unrelated belts (Figure~\ref{fig:d2}); their silhouettes (0.516 and 0.334)
still beat fake data (0.319 and 0.295), so the score confirms that the data are not random, not that the
clusters are meaningful. GMM, chosen by BIC, uses all 50 components the grid allows: BIC keeps rewarding
smaller ellipses along the belts, so it gives no natural number of clusters.

\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{earthquakes_2023_clusters}
\caption{D2 at the chosen settings (grey: noise). HDBSCAN follows the plate belts; DBSCAN joins the
dense western Pacific into one cluster; K-means and Spectral draw six regions that each span several
belts; GMM splits the belts into 50 pieces.}
\label{fig:d2}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{A dense city with no gaps}\label{sec:city}
% Sources: results/datasets/uber_pickups_results.csv; figure uber_pickups_clusters.png.
D3 has density contrast but no gaps (Figure~\ref{fig:d3}): Manhattan, Brooklyn and Queens meet without empty space, and only
the airports stand apart. Both density methods therefore keep the city together. DBSCAN returns the city
(97.7\% of pickups) and JFK; HDBSCAN returns the city (91.5\%), JFK and LaGuardia with 4.6\% noise. DBSCAN
has the top silhouette on D3 (0.757) because two well-separated groups score well whatever their sizes.
HDBSCAN scores below its setting on fake data (0.638 against 0.660), and so does Spectral (0.157 against
0.308). The $k$-based methods divide the core: K-Means++ into five regions (silhouette 0.481 against
0.338 on fake data) and GMM into nine. Their borders follow their objectives, not gaps in the data.

separate only the airports; K-means, GMM and Spectral divide the core along borders of their own making.

\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Rare classes in seven dimensions}\label{sec:rare}
% Sources: results/datasets/ecoli_results.csv; ecoli_tuning.csv (best-ARI settings).
D4 has eight localisation sites, three of them with 2 to 5 proteins, in seven dimensions with no density
gaps between classes. The methods that model compact groups do best: GMM reaches ARI 0.618, K-Means++
0.508 and Spectral 0.392 at $K=8$. DBSCAN reaches 0.395 only by calling 31.5\% of proteins noise. HDBSCAN
puts 97.0\% of proteins in one cluster (ARI 0.038), yet has the highest silhouette on D4 (0.559), no
better than the same setting on fake data (0.553). With the labels the gap narrows only for the
$k$-based methods: GMM reaches 0.741 (7 spherical components) and K-Means++ 0.731 ($K=7$), against 0.398
for HDBSCAN (29.8\% noise) and 0.395 for DBSCAN.

\subsubsection{High dimensions}\label{sec:dims}
% Sources: results/datasets/fashion_mnist_results.csv; fashion_mnist_tuning.csv (best-ARI settings).
On D5 ($d=50$) the $k$-based methods do best: Spectral (ARI 0.398), GMM (0.389) and K-Means++ (0.343), all
well short of the labels because shirts, T-shirts, pullovers and coats overlap. Both density methods
collapse to two clusters: DBSCAN puts 75.7\% of images in one and 22.7\% in noise (ARI 0.028), HDBSCAN
66.3\% in one and 32.9\% in noise (ARI 0.023). This is not a bad choice of setting: the best density
settings in the grid reach only 0.034 and 0.066. In 50 dimensions the distances to the $k$-th neighbour
are similar for every point, so no single density threshold separates the overlapping classes.
HDBSCAN's silhouette (0.039) is below its setting on fake data (0.229).

\subsubsection{No gaps between groups}\label{sec:nogaps}
% Sources: results/datasets/hdb_resale_results.csv; figure hdb_resale_clusters.png.
D8 is one continuous mass: flats come in every combination of size, lease, storey and price.
Table~\ref{tab:cmp_unlabelled} shows what each method does with it (Figure~\ref{fig:d8}). The density
methods have no sparse region to cut along: DBSCAN and HDBSCAN put 99.7\% of sales in one cluster and
leave fewer than 100 outlying sales outside it. Their silhouettes (0.438 and 0.496) are the highest on D8 and
well above fake data (0.189 and 0.136), because a giant cluster plus a tiny distant one scores well.
K-means finds four segments with a silhouette of 0.318 against 0.216 on fake data, a weak but real
partition; Spectral leaves 67.1\% of sales in one of its four clusters, and GMM spreads ten components
over the mass. On data with no gaps a density method has nothing to find, and neither the silhouette nor
the fake-data check says so; only the share of the largest cluster does.

\subsubsection{Silhouette versus ground truth}\label{sec:silhouette}
% Sources: results/datasets/{make_moons,ecoli,fashion_mnist,wine}_results.csv. Spearman over the five
% methods (K-Means++, DBSCAN, HDBSCAN, GMM, Spectral).
On every labelled dataset the method with the highest silhouette is not the one that matches the labels
best (Table~\ref{tab:cmp_labelled}): on D1 HDBSCAN (0.529, ARI 0.709) against Spectral (ARI 1.000); on D4
HDBSCAN (0.559, ARI 0.038) against GMM (0.618); on D5 K-Means++ (0.202, ARI 0.343) against Spectral
(0.398); on D6 DBSCAN (0.365, ARI 0.431) against GMM (0.915). Over the five methods the rank correlation
between silhouette and ARI is $-0.30$ on D1, $-0.70$ on D4, $-0.60$ on D6 and $0.50$ on D5, the only
dataset with no density method in the lead. Two mechanisms recur. The silhouette ignores noise, so a
density method can raise it by discarding hard points (D6 DBSCAN, 23.6\% noise). It also rewards one
giant cluster plus a small distant one (D4 HDBSCAN, 97.0\% in one cluster).

\habegin
\paragraph{D6 Wine and the fake-data check.} \hatag{}
% Source: results/datasets/wine_results.csv.
On D6, DBSCAN and HDBSCAN pass the noise cap with 23.6\% and 42.1\% of the wines as noise, take the two
highest silhouettes (0.365 and 0.363) and the two lowest ARI (0.431 and 0.260), while GMM and K-means
reach 0.915 and 0.897 with silhouettes near 0.28. The fake-data check does not separate good from bad
here either. Every method that assigns all points scores far above its shuffled-column silhouette (0.285
against 0.066 for K-means), and the density methods form no cluster at all on shuffled data. So each
result passes the check, including the density results that match the labels worst: what they find, a
dense core of one or two cultivars, is real structure, but it is not the class structure. The check
shows that a result is not an artefact of the marginals. It does not show which of several real
structures a method has found.
\haend

\paragraph{What the fake-data check catches.} Across the seven datasets a setting scores no better than
on fake data in six cases: DBSCAN on D1 (0.523 against 0.822) and D2 (0.420 against 0.599), HDBSCAN on D3,
D4 and D5, and Spectral on D3. The three on labelled data are the clear failures by ARI (D1 DBSCAN, D4
and D5 HDBSCAN), so the check catches results with no real structure. The check misses the D8 density results, which beat fake data while holding
99.7\% of sales in one cluster, and the K-means regions on D2, which beat fake data while joining
unrelated belts.

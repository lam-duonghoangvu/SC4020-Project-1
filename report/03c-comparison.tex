\subsubsection{Curved and elongated clusters}\label{sec:shape}
% Lam: results/datasets/make_moons_results.csv
On D1 the centroid methods fail and the graph and density methods succeed. K-Means++ (ARI 0.481) and
GMM (0.489) both draw a straight boundary that cuts each moon in two, giving the tip of one moon to
the other cluster (Figure~\ref{fig:lam-d1}). Spectral clustering with a 10-nearest-neighbour graph
recovers both moons exactly (ARI 1.000), DBSCAN nearly so (0.982, 0.9\% noise) and HDBSCAN slightly
less (0.947, 2.7\% noise, mostly the sparse moon tips). Silhouette ranks the methods the other way
round: K-Means++ and GMM score 0.495 against 0.387 for Spectral, because silhouette rewards compact,
convex groups, which is exactly the wrong assumption here.

% Source: experiments/09_usgs_earthquakes_2023_ablation.ipynb, section 5 (K-means belt case, printed
% centre distances); figure from the same cell.
K-means assumes compact clusters, and D2 breaks that assumption at planetary scale. Earthquakes follow
plate boundaries, so a cluster is a long,
narrow belt rather than a ball around a centre. K-Means++ with $K=6$ must cover the whole globe with six
roughly round regions, so each region takes in several unrelated belts
(Figure~\ref{fig:kmeans_belts}). One cluster of 905 earthquakes joins Turkey, the Reykjanes Ridge and
Iceland, Afghanistan and Tajikistan, Somalia and Eritrea, and the Mid-Indian Ridge. HDBSCAN finds each of
these as its own cluster, and their centres are 2{,}932 to 11{,}449\,km apart. K-means also has no noise
label, so every isolated earthquake in the oceans is assigned to some region. Its silhouette is still
0.528, well above the 0.391 of shuffled coordinates: the score confirms that the data are not random, not
that the clusters are meaningful.

\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{eq_case_kmeans_merges_belts}
\caption{D2, failure of K-Means++ ($K=6$). All points shown in colour or dark grey belong to one K-means
cluster; colours are the HDBSCAN clusters inside it, crosses their centres. Cause: cluster shape. Belts
thousands of kilometres apart are joined because K-means can only draw compact regions.}
\label{fig:kmeans_belts}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Uneven density}\label{sec:density}
% Sources: results/earthquakes_2023/case_density_regions.csv, method_comparison.csv;
% k-distance spread from experiments/07_usgs_earthquakes_2023_eda.ipynb, section 12.
On D2 the distance to the nearest earthquake runs from 1.2\,km to 100.5\,km (5th to 95th percentile), and
the k-distance curve rises smoothly with no knee. Any single $\varepsilon$ is therefore too small for some
belts and too large for others. At the chosen $\varepsilon=101.8$\,km, DBSCAN clusters 90\% of the
earthquakes in the dense south-west Pacific but only 20\% in the sparser Americas
(Figure~\ref{fig:density}). HDBSCAN, which picks a density level per cluster, clusters 62\% of the
Americas and keeps the Mexico to Panama belt and the Chile and Argentina belt as long clusters. The price is paid in the dense
region, where it clusters 78\% instead of 90\%. Overall HDBSCAN keeps more earthquakes (69.3\% against
59.6\%) with a higher silhouette (0.757 against 0.703, Table~\ref{tab:cmp_d2d8}).

\begin{figure}[h]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{eq_case_density_dbscan_vs_hdbscan}
\caption{D2, DBSCAN ($\varepsilon=101.8$\,km) against HDBSCAN in a sparse region (top, the Americas)
and a dense one (bottom, the south-west Pacific); grey points are noise. Success of HDBSCAN on the sparse
belts, at the cost of trimming the dense ones. Cause: density varies along and between belts.}
\label{fig:density}
\end{figure}
% [Ky] Sources: results/hotspot/case_stats.csv (cluster shares and centres), sweep.csv (knee setting),
% shared_rule.csv (chosen settings). Hoang Anh: please check against your D3 run.
On D3 density varies as much, but inside one connected mass. Lower and midtown Manhattan, the rest of
Manhattan, Brooklyn and western Queens meet without a gap, so the density methods keep them together.
DBSCAN at the knee of the k-distance curve ($\varepsilon=0.525$\,km) returns 13 clusters, yet 91.6\% of
pickups sit in the first; the others are the two airports and small spots in Jersey City, Astoria, Harlem
and Brooklyn, and the silhouette is 0.04. The rule prefers $\varepsilon=1.58$\,km: two clusters, the city
(96.1\%) and JFK, with a silhouette of 0.779 (Table~\ref{tab:cmp_d3d4}). HDBSCAN finds the city, JFK and
LaGuardia (91.2\%, 2.0\% and 1.9\%; 4.8\% noise). The airports are the only parts of D3 with a gap around
them, and both density methods find them. K-means ($K=5$) does divide the core, into southern Manhattan
(44.5\%), northern Manhattan with the Bronx (39.9\%) and Brooklyn (9.3\%), plus the two airport areas, but
those borders follow the K-means objective, not gaps in the data. Its silhouette is 0.475 against 0.362
on shuffled coordinates, so the split is not random, but nothing in the data marks where it should fall.

\subsubsection{High dimensions}\label{sec:dims}
% [Ky] Sources: results/hotspot/ablation.csv (dimensionality), shared_rule.csv.
D4 has only seven dimensions, but it already shows the effect. Given its best $\varepsilon$ in each case,
DBSCAN beats K-Means++ on the first two and three principal components (ARI 0.575 and 0.618 against 0.468
and 0.466) and loses from four components on, by 0.276 on all seven (0.455 against 0.731; Ablation~D).
Without labels the density methods do far worse. On all seven features the rule picks settings where one
cluster holds 97\% of proteins, with ARI 0.04 (Table~\ref{tab:cmp_d3d4}), and their silhouettes (0.560
and 0.559) are barely above the same settings on shuffled columns (0.548 and 0.551). The fake-data check
flags both. K-means at the $K=5$ the silhouette picks reaches ARI 0.684 to 0.697.
% Lam: results/datasets/fashion_mnist_results.csv
On D5 ($d=50$) the ranking reverses from D1. The $k$-based methods do best: Spectral (ARI 0.398),
GMM (0.391) and K-Means++ (0.343), all well short of the labels because shirts, T-shirts, pullovers
and coats overlap. DBSCAN finds two clusters and 33\% noise (ARI 0.033): one cluster absorbs almost
every class (Figure~\ref{fig:lam-d5}). HDBSCAN finds 28 small clusters but labels 71\% of points
noise (ARI 0.050). No setting in the HDBSCAN grid met the 50\% noise limit, because in 50 dimensions
the k-distances of all points are similar and there is no density gap between the overlapping
classes for one threshold to find.

\subsubsection{No gaps between groups}\label{sec:nogaps}
% Sources: results/hdb_resale/comparison.csv, kmeans_k_sweep.csv.
D8 is one continuous mass: flats come in every combination of size, lease, storey and price, with no empty
space between groups. Table~\ref{tab:cmp_d2d8} shows what each method does with it. K-means still finds a
weak partition, four market segments (newer mid-size, older small, large older, newer high-floor), with a
silhouette of 0.317 against 0.218 on shuffled columns. That ratio of 1.45 is modest, but it is the only
result on D8 that beats its fake data. Density methods have no sparse region to cut along. The DBSCAN
setting chosen by the rule puts 97.1\% of sales in one cluster, and HDBSCAN finds one core holding 68.6\%
of sales plus a compact block of new 3-room flats, and calls 28.5\% noise. Both score below the same
setting on shuffled columns (0.239 against 0.330, and 0.160 against 0.593). On shuffled columns HDBSCAN calls
94.0\% of sales noise and keeps two small, well-separated clusters, which the silhouette rewards. On data
with no gaps, a density method has nothing to find, and the selection rule
cannot tell.

\subsubsection{Silhouette versus ground truth}\label{sec:silhouette}
% Lam: results/datasets/wine_results.csv, results/datasets/fashion_mnist_results.csv
On D6, DBSCAN has the best silhouette (0.42 against 0.28 for K-Means++) but a far worse ARI (0.31
against 0.90): it scores only the 35\% of points it keeps and drops the other 65\% as noise. On D5,
HDBSCAN also has the top silhouette (0.228), computed on the 29\% of points it does not call noise,
and an ARI of only 0.050. In both cases silhouette excludes noise, so a method can raise it by
discarding the hard points.

\paragraph{D6 Wine and the fake-data check.} \hatag{}
% [Hoang Anh]: from my run; Lam's run of D6 gives the same picture with other density settings.
% Source: results/comparison/report_d6.csv.
On D6, DBSCAN and HDBSCAN pass the noise cap with about a third of the
wines as noise, take the two highest silhouettes (0.356 and 0.352) and the two lowest ARI (0.296 and
0.342), while GMM and K-means reach 0.915 and 0.897 with silhouettes near 0.28. The fake-data check does
not separate good from bad here either. Every method that assigns all points scores far above its
shuffled-column silhouette (0.285 against 0.066 for K-means; Table~\ref{tab:d6}), and the density methods form no cluster at all on shuffled data. So each result passes the
check, including the density results that match the labels worst: what they find, a dense core of one
class, is real structure, but it is not the class structure. The check shows that a result is not an
artefact of the marginals. It does not show which of several real structures a method has found.
